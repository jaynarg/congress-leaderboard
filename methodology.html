How this is measured

Every number here comes from the Congress.gov API, published by the Library of Congress, and is recomputed nightly. Nothing is hand-entered or editorially weighted.

What counts as a bill

Rankings count bills and joint resolutions (HR, S, HJRES, SJRES), which are the measures that can become law. Simple and concurrent resolutions, which mostly express sentiment or handle internal chamber business, appear on member pages but are kept out of the rankings so that commemorative measures don't inflate a total.

What counts as bipartisan

A sponsored bill is bipartisan here if at least one member of the other caucus signed on as an original cosponsor, meaning they were there at introduction rather than added later. Recruiting a single member of the other party after the fact is common practice, so the stricter test is the more meaningful one. Member pages also show how many cosponsors across all of a member's bills came from the other party, and what share of a member's own cosponsorships went to the other party's bills.

Cosponsorships that were later withdrawn are excluded everywhere. Independents count with the party they caucus with, and party is evaluated as of the date a bill was introduced or a cosponsorship was signed, so a member who switched parties mid-Congress is counted correctly on both sides of the switch.

Where the summaries come from

Each bill shows the Congressional Research Service summary when one exists. Most bills do not have one for weeks or months after introduction, and many never get one. In those cases the site shows a summary written by Claude, Anthropic's language model, from the text of the bill itself, marked AI summary. These are generated from the bill text alone, with instructions to describe only what the measure would do and to avoid any characterization of its merits. They are replaced automatically once a Research Service summary is published. Bills whose text has not yet been posted show no summary at all.

How far a bill got

Out of committee
Reported by committee or discharged from it.
Passed a chamber
Passed or agreed to in the House or the Senate.
Became law
Signed by the President, or enacted without signature. A few bills are signed before their public law number is assigned; those are counted as law.

Stage counts are cumulative: a bill that became law also counts as having passed and as having come out of committee.

Known limits

  • Bills folded into larger packages are undercounted. Much lawmaking happens when a member's text is absorbed into an appropriations bill, the defense authorization, or a reconciliation package. The standalone bill then dies on paper while the policy becomes law. Only the standalone path is measured here.
  • Position shapes these numbers as much as skill does. Majority-party members and committee chairs advance more bills by virtue of the gavel. Leadership often sponsors very little. Senators and representatives are not comparable, which is why the two chambers are ranked separately.
  • Sponsorship is not the whole job. Several members show zero sponsored bills and are still central to what passes, through committee work, negotiation, or floor management.
  • A higher number is not automatically better. Volume can mean message bills as easily as it means workhorse legislating.
